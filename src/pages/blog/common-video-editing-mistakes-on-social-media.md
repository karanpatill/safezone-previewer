Most problems with vertical video layout are not creative problems. They come from editing on a screen that shows nothing of the interface the video will be watched through. Here are the mistakes that come up again and again, and what to do instead.

## 1. Subtitles in the traditional lower third

Broadcast and YouTube conventions put subtitles near the bottom of the frame. On a vertical video that is exactly where the caption, handle and audio row live. Subtitles at y 1600 on a 1920-tall frame will be covered on every major app.

**Fix:** set the bottom subtitle baseline between y 1180 and y 1420. That is still visually “low” on a phone, but it clears TikTok’s caption block, the strictest of the three.

## 2. Text that runs edge to edge

Wide subtitle lines look balanced in an editor, but on a phone the right-hand action rail covers x 930–1080 from roughly the middle of the frame down. A line that spans the full width will lose its last word behind the like count.

**Fix:** limit lines to about 32 characters and centre a narrow block. If a sentence is long, break it into two short lines rather than one wide one.

## 3. Headlines in the top 285 pixels

A hook at the very top of the frame feels natural, and it is visible in the full-screen Reels and TikTok players. But when a Reel autoplays in the Instagram feed it is cropped to 4:5, and the top 285 px are cut off. Your hook becomes half a line of text.

**Fix:** start headlines at y 300 or lower. Turn on the 4:5 layer in the [previewer](/#tool) to see the feed crop on your own frame.

## 4. Corner logos and watermarks

Agencies often place a logo in a corner as they would on a TV spot. On vertical video every corner is occupied: the top corners by the status bar, title and camera or search icons, the bottom corners by the handle and the action rail.

**Fix:** if you need a logo, place it top-left just inside the safe zone (around x 80, y 240), or build the brand into the content itself, such as on a product, a lower-third card placed high, or a branded end card.

## 5. “Link below” arrows

An arrow pointing down at “the link below” points at the platform’s interface, not your link. On Reels and TikTok the bottom of the frame is covered by the caption; the link, if there is one, is in the bio or a sticker.

**Fix:** say where the link actually is (“link in bio”) and place that text inside the safe zone. Remove downward-pointing graphics entirely.

## 6. Framing faces in the right third

The rule of thirds suggests placing subjects on the left or right vertical line. On vertical video the right line, at x 720, sits next to the action rail, and in the lower half of the frame a subject there gets buttons over their shoulder.

**Fix:** centre faces, or bias them slightly left. Keep the eyes around one third of the way down the frame, around y 640, which also keeps them inside the 4:5 and 3:4 crops.

## 7. Designing one cover for every surface

The cover frame of a Reel is shown in several shapes: full 9:16 in the player, 4:5 in the feed and 3:4 on your profile grid since January 2025. A cover title near the top of the 9:16 frame is cut off on the grid.

**Fix:** keep cover titles inside the central 1080 × 1440, ideally in the middle third. Turn on the 3:4 layer when you check a cover.

## 8. Exporting at the wrong size

Uploading 1080 × 1350 or 1080 × 1080 video to a vertical player gets it letterboxed or scaled up, and uploading 720 × 1280 makes it soft. Some editors also export at 1080 × 1920 with accidental black bars because the sequence settings did not match the source.

**Fix:** set your sequence to 1080 × 1920 (or 2160 × 3840 for 4K) at the start of the edit, and check the exported file’s dimensions. The previewer’s media panel shows the resolution and warns when a file is not 9:16.

## 9. Trusting a single template forever

Platforms update their interfaces several times a year, test variants on subsets of users, and add rows such as shop links, collaboration handles or translations. A template you downloaded two years ago may no longer match.

**Fix:** treat every safe zone as a minimum, keep a 40–60 px buffer, and recheck your template when a platform ships a visible redesign. We review the SafeZoneSnap presets whenever that happens.
